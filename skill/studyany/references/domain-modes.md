# Domain Modes

The coach uses one learning loop but selects teaching activities and evidence
that match the kind of ability being developed. Identify the dominant mode;
combine modes when the goal genuinely spans them.

## Conceptual and theoretical

Use for science, history, law as education, economics, definitions, and
frameworks.

Activities:

- retrieve definitions and relationships from memory;
- explain a mechanism in the learner's own words;
- compare similar concepts and separate them with counterexamples;
- reconstruct a timeline, causal chain, proof, or argument;
- apply the idea to a new case.

Evidence:

- accurate explanation without notes;
- correct use of key terms;
- justified comparison or causal argument;
- performance on an unfamiliar example.

Do not reward copying a summary. Ask for reconstruction and application.

## Procedural and technical

Use for programming, software tools, laboratory procedures, and professional
workflows.

Activities:

- inspect a worked example and predict the result;
- complete missing steps;
- perform the procedure with a checklist;
- troubleshoot a deliberately flawed example;
- complete an independent task with changed inputs.

Evidence:

- an executable result, artifact, or completed procedure;
- explanation of important decisions;
- ability to diagnose a nearby failure;
- repeatable performance without step-by-step prompting.

When tools are available, ask the learner to run or inspect the result. Never
claim that code or a procedure worked without evidence.

## Problem-solving and mathematics

Use for mathematics, algorithms, puzzles, diagnosis, and decision-making.

Activities:

- restate constraints and known information;
- decompose the problem;
- predict which strategy may work and why;
- solve a small instance before the full problem;
- compare two solutions and analyze failure cases;
- solve a variant with changed constraints.

Evidence:

- correct result;
- valid strategy selection;
- reasoning that survives a changed example;
- awareness of assumptions, edge cases, or complexity.

Give strategy-level hints before algebraic or code-level answers.

## Language learning

Use for natural languages, pronunciation, vocabulary, grammar, reading,
listening, speaking, and writing.

Activities:

- retrieve words or structures in context;
- alternate recognition with production;
- use short comprehensible input followed by output;
- correct one or two high-value errors at a time;
- revisit vocabulary in varied sentences;
- use role-play or a realistic communication task.

Evidence:

- delayed recall;
- understandable production;
- correct meaning and register in context;
- ability to understand or produce a new example.

Do not count seeing a word as being able to use it. Label corrections as
meaning, grammar, pronunciation, or register.

## Creative and expressive

Use for writing, design, music, visual art, and presentation skills.

Activities:

- analyze a strong example and identify choices;
- imitate one constrained technique;
- produce a small artifact;
- use a visible rubric for revision;
- create a variation under a new constraint;
- reflect on the decision process.

Evidence:

- an artifact or performance;
- deliberate use of the target technique;
- revision based on a criterion;
- ability to explain choices and respond to a new brief.

Avoid judging taste as a universal fact. Separate objective constraints from
style preferences.

## Practical and physical skills

Use for cooking, crafts, equipment operation, public speaking, and supervised
physical skills.

Activities:

- observe or read a safe demonstration;
- practice one component at low complexity;
- use a checklist and inspect the result;
- increase constraints only after stable performance;
- reflect on safety, quality, and repeatability.

Evidence:

- a real artifact, performance, or observed procedure;
- correct setup and safety checks;
- repeatable execution;
- ability to detect and correct a common fault.

For dangerous, medical, or high-consequence activities, do not substitute AI
instructions for qualified supervision. Prefer general educational guidance,
conservative boundaries, and authoritative local resources.

## Mixed-domain goals

Many goals combine modes. For example, learning a technical profession may
require concepts, procedures, problem-solving, and communication. Build a
small mode plan instead of forcing everything into one activity:

```text
concept -> guided procedure -> independent problem -> explanation or artifact
```

Keep the final evidence tied to the learner's actual goal.
