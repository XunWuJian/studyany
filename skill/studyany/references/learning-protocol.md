# Learning Protocol

This reference defines the general protocol behind every subject. Use it to
turn a vague learning request into a sequence of observable actions and
evidence. The protocol is adaptive: keep the loop stable while changing the
content, examples, difficulty, and evidence type for the domain. For pacing,
long-term retention, and supportive correction, also read
`references/learning-regulation.md`.

## 1. Define the target

Ask what the learner wants to do, not only what topic they want to cover.
Convert the answer into an observable target with a context, performance, and
quality bar:

```text
In <context>, independently <performance> to <quality bar>.
```

Examples:

- In a work meeting, summarize a technical proposal and identify two risks.
- Given a new problem, choose and justify an appropriate algorithm.
- After a week without notes, explain the causes of an event and compare two
  interpretations.

Keep the original wording as motivation, but use the observable version for
planning and assessment.

## 2. Establish a baseline

Use the smallest diagnostic that distinguishes likely starting points. Prefer
three to ten items and include at least one item that requires application.
Collect confidence before the answer when practical. Classify errors as one or
more of:

- missing prerequisite;
- misconception;
- procedure or strategy selection;
- execution or careless error;
- retrieval failure;
- transfer failure;
- unclear prompt or missing context.

Do not turn the diagnostic into a graded exam unless the learner asks for one.
Use it to select the first lesson and mark uncertainty in the roadmap.

## 3. Build a provisional roadmap

Represent a roadmap as a dependency graph or ordered stages. Each stage needs:

- a purpose related to the goal;
- concepts or subskills;
- prerequisite links;
- practice evidence;
- an exit check;
- a fallback if the learner fails.

Prefer the smallest complete path to the goal. Offer optional depth separately.
Do not teach every topic associated with a subject when the learner's goal only
needs a subset.

Use milestone gates rather than calendar promises. A stage is ready to advance
when the evidence meets its exit criteria; time estimates are provisional.

### Challenge review

When the learner disputes a fact, procedure, route, assessment, or question,
pause the roadmap change and use `references/challenge-protocol.md`. State the
exact claim and assumptions, check the strongest available evidence, and select
one verdict. A factual error is corrected and propagated; a valid alternative
is compared while one default remains explicit; an ambiguous question is
voided; and evidence that cannot decide is deferred. Repeated rejection
without new evidence does not justify alternating methods or restarting the
roadmap.

## 4. Run a study session

Use this default allocation as a starting point, not a rigid law:

```text
15-25%: retrieval of due or recently learned material
15-25%: new explanation and worked example
35-55%: guided and independent practice
10-20%: feedback, correction, and exit check
```

Short sessions can omit explanation when review evidence is strong. Long
sessions should include a break and a second retrieval attempt rather than
one uninterrupted explanation. A same-session correction check is useful, but
it is not a substitute for delayed retrieval. Do not use extra same-session
repetitions to claim durable retention.

Before changing pace or tone, run the trigger gate in
`references/learning-regulation.md`. If no observable trigger is present, do
not infer a psychological state and continue the normal lesson loop.

### Objective format

State:

```text
By the end of this session, you will be able to <observable action>.
Evidence: <answer, artifact, performance, or explanation>.
```

Choose no more than two primary objectives unless the learner explicitly asks
for a survey.

### Select the evidence medium

After defining the objective and evidence, choose the smallest medium that can
show the target performance:

- `conversation`: recall, explanation, comparison, reasoning, planning, or
  feedback can be completed in the dialogue;
- `artifact`: the learner must edit, run, manipulate, produce, or observe
  something outside the dialogue;
- `mixed`: the session needs both a short dialogue exchange and an external
  result.

For `artifact` or `mixed`, read `references/artifact-workflow.md`. State the
workspace, artifact, learner action, and review evidence before the learner
starts. Use existing learner material when possible and create only a minimal
non-destructive starter when needed.

### Retrieval first

Ask a question, prediction, blank, explanation, or short task before providing
the relevant explanation. It is acceptable to provide a scaffold when the
learner has no entry point. Record whether the answer was unaided, hinted, or
shown.

### Explanation

Explain only the concept needed for the next task. Use a concrete example,
counterexample, and connection to prior knowledge when useful. Check for
misconceptions with a question instead of asking only whether the explanation
was clear.

### Practice ladder

Use the following progression:

1. Worked example: the coach demonstrates and narrates decisions.
2. Completion task: the learner fills missing decisions or steps.
3. Guided task: the learner works with hints available.
4. Independent task: the learner works without the example.
5. Transfer task: the learner handles a changed context or constraint.

Stop at the level appropriate to the learner's evidence. Do not make every
session difficult for its own sake.

### Feedback

Give feedback that identifies:

- what was correct and why;
- the first consequential error;
- the concept or decision involved;
- one correction strategy;
- a retry or a nearby variation.

Avoid replacing the learner's entire attempt with an answer. A full solution
is useful after an attempt, when a safety issue exists, or when the learner
explicitly asks for a worked solution.

## 5. Exit check

End with one to three checks. At least one should be unaided. Use a different
example from the explanation when checking application. Record the result as
evidence, not as a personality judgment.

Possible exit evidence:

- a correct explanation with an example;
- a completed procedure or artifact;
- a solution plus strategy justification;
- a short oral or written production;
- a transfer task under a new constraint.

If the learner cannot finish, record partial evidence and the smallest next
step. Do not mark the objective complete merely because the lesson was read.

Give one evidence-based progress, lag, or next-evidence statement before
closing. Then schedule the next *spaced* review. The same-session exit check
can confirm correction, but it cannot replace a retrieval attempt after a
meaningful interval.

## 6. Adaptation rules

Use evidence to adjust the next action:

- repeated prerequisite errors: branch back to the prerequisite;
- correct with hints but not independently: keep the concept and reduce
  scaffolding gradually;
- fast independent success: add a transfer task or proceed to the next gate;
- high confidence with low performance: add prediction and delayed retrieval;
- low confidence with high performance: show evidence and increase challenge
  gradually without dismissing the learner's concern;
- repeated careless errors: shorten tasks, add checking routines, and inspect
  workload or attention before adding more content;
- repeated forgetting after intervals: shorten the interval and vary retrieval
  cues.

When the learner is working for a long block, making rapid gains without
delayed evidence, repeating an error, explicitly reporting strain, or drifting
from the target, apply the smallest response in
`references/learning-regulation.md`. Do not infer a state when none of these
signals is observable.

## 7. Weekly adjustment

Once per week, compare planned work with evidence:

- What was learned and retained?
- Which errors recur?
- Is the schedule realistic?
- Is the learner practicing the target performance or only adjacent content?
- Which stage should be continued, revisited, or removed?
- Which learning-regulation signals occurred, and did the adjustment help?

Compare retrieval, application, transfer, and retention separately. Increased
minutes or confidence alone is not a positive learning curve. Change one or
two variables at a time so the learner can tell what helped.
