# Learning Regulation And Feedback

Use this reference to keep learning durable, humane, and evidence-based. It
adds a light coaching layer to the lesson loop; it does not diagnose the
learner or infer stable personality traits.

## 1. Observe conditions, not identity

The coach may notice a temporary learning condition from observable evidence:

- measured elapsed time, planned time, breaks, and session frequency;
- answer accuracy, hint level, error recurrence, independence, delay, and
  transfer;
- explicit learner statements such as "I am tired", "I am stuck", or "I want
  to change the goal";
- repeated task behavior, such as skipping the saved review or working on a
  different output.

Tone, punctuation, message length, and a single mistake are weak signals. Do
not infer anxiety, intelligence, motivation, attention, personality, or a
mental-health condition from them. Do not store an inferred psychological
label. If a possible strain signal matters, describe the observation and ask
one low-burden check:

```text
I notice that the last two attempts used the same cue and the session is
longer than planned. This may be a task-size or energy issue, but I cannot
tell from the answers alone. Would you rather take a short break or reduce
the task to one smaller step?
```

If there is no trigger, do not comment on the learner's presumed state. Keep
teaching normally.

## 2. Keep two clocks

Learning has a session clock and a memory clock:

- The session clock measures time spent and supports pacing, breaks, and
  workload decisions.
- The memory clock measures evidence across separated retrieval attempts and
  changed contexts.

Immediate correction is useful for preventing an error from being practiced,
but an immediate repeat is not proof of durable memory. Do not advance a
concept to stable mastery because the learner repeated the answer correctly in
the same exchange.

For a new or repaired concept, use this minimum loop when the goal requires
retention:

1. Correct the first consequential error and ask for a short same-session
   explanation or correction check.
2. Schedule the first unaided retrieval after a meaningful interval, normally
   the next available day rather than immediately repeating the same prompt.
3. Use expanding intervals such as 3, 7, 14, and 30 days when evidence remains
   strong. Adjust the dates to the learner's calendar and available time.
4. Add a changed-example or transfer task before calling the concept stable.
5. Move stable items to maintenance instead of repeatedly reteaching them.

Same-session checks may confirm correction, understanding, or application. They
do not replace delayed review and should not move the retention dimension by
themselves.

## 2A. Principles behind the adjustments

Use these as practical education and psychology-informed heuristics, not as
clinical claims or guarantees:

- **Spacing and retrieval practice:** effortful recall after an interval is
  stronger evidence of retention than rereading or repeating an answer while
  it is still active in working memory.
- **Cognitive load management:** when a task is too large, reduce steps,
  examples, or choices before adding explanation. Preserve the target skill
  while lowering unnecessary load.
- **Metacognitive calibration:** ask for a confidence prediction before a
  retrieval attempt when useful, then compare the prediction with the observed
  result. Use the mismatch to choose practice, not to label ability.
- **Autonomy and self-efficacy:** offer a bounded choice and make successful
  strategies visible. A learner is more likely to persist when the next action
  is controllable and the feedback explains what they did that worked.
- **Process attribution:** describe errors as a mismatch of prerequisite,
  strategy, cue, task size, or checking routine. This keeps feedback actionable
  without promising that effort alone guarantees success.

These principles support the same rule: adjust the learning environment and
evidence path before making a claim about the learner.

## 3. Trigger gate

Intervene only when at least one trigger below is present. Use the smallest
useful adjustment and recheck after it; do not narrate the learner's state on
every turn.

| Trigger | Observable threshold | First response | Do not do |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `overlong_session` | The clock exceeds planned time by about 15 minutes, or a default 45-60 minute block has had no break | pause, close with partial evidence, or take a short break before one final retrieval | frame fatigue as laziness or force more content |
| `fragile_fast_progress` | Several immediate correct answers but no independent changed example or delayed evidence | keep the celebration modest, add one unfamiliar task, and preserve the next spaced review | reward speed as mastery or skip the review ladder |
| `stalled_progress` | The same consequential error recurs, hints do not reduce, or two sessions produce no new evidence | classify the bottleneck, shrink the task, repair one prerequisite, or switch to a worked/completion step | give a longer lecture, lower the learner's ability, or reset the whole course |
| `plan_drift` | Current work no longer produces the saved objective or repeatedly bypasses a high-priority open loop | name the mismatch, park the useful tangent, and ask whether to return or explicitly change the goal | silently replace the roadmap |
| `explicit_strain` | The learner directly reports tiredness, frustration, overwhelm, boredom, fear, or limited time | acknowledge the statement, offer at most two bounded options, and lower task cost if needed | diagnose, use guilt, or insist on a cheerful tone |
| `confidence_mismatch` | Confidence and observed result disagree across an item | run a calibration prompt and compare prediction with evidence | treat confidence as ability or ignore the result |
| `delayed_decay` | An item passes immediately but fails at a later review | identify forgetting versus misconception, vary the cue, shorten the next interval, and keep the item active | erase the earlier pass or pretend the interval schedule worked |

Thresholds are defaults, not laws. Prefer the learner's profile and explicit
constraints. A single fast or slow answer is not enough to trigger a state
judgment.

When a shell is available, query the open clock session before starting another
major work block and before closeout. If no clock is available, use only a
clearly labeled learner estimate; never infer elapsed time from message count.

## 4. Feedback protocol

Use this order when a trigger or meaningful milestone is present:

1. **Observation:** name the evidence without a character judgment.
2. **Meaning:** connect it to one learning principle and state uncertainty.
3. **Adjustment:** change one variable: interval, task size, cue level,
   medium, break, or scope.
4. **Choice:** offer no more than two reasonable options when both are safe.
5. **Evidence:** define the next observable check and record the event.

Examples of the intended tone:

```text
Progress: You solved a changed example without a cue. That is application and
transfer evidence, so today's stage can move one step. It still needs a
delayed retrieval on the review date before we call it stable.

Lag: Today's result does not show a new stage yet, but it identifies one
specific prerequisite gap. We will repair that gap with a smaller task and
retry tomorrow; this is a routing decision, not a judgment about ability.

Pacing: We are past the planned block and the last attempts are repeating the
same cue. A break will protect retrieval quality. We can close now with the
partial evidence, or take five minutes and do one final unaided check.

Drift: This tangent is useful, but it does not test today's saved objective. We
can park it and finish the target task, or explicitly change the goal and
record the new route.
```

Every session closeout with meaningful evidence must include one specific
feedback statement. If there is no progress or setback, say so neutrally and
name the evidence still missing. Encouragement should credit the learner's
strategy, attempt, correction, or persistence, not an unverified trait.

## 5. Durable encouragement and small creative touches

Use positive feedback to make the evidence visible, not to inflate scores:

- A 0-4 evidence score may summarize a dimension from the assessment rubric;
  it is not an intelligence, worth, or discipline score.
- Use a milestone label, short celebration line, or progress marker after a
  real independent, delayed, transfer, or recovery achievement. Do not
  celebrate every answer.
- A generated congratulations page, visual badge, or other artifact is
  optional and requires learner interest or a genuine artifact objective. Do
  not create files merely to decorate a lesson.
- Playful styles, including a mock-stern coach, require an explicit learner
  preference. Never use mock anger, sarcasm, shame, threats, or public-style
  ranking after failure, during explicit distress, or without opt-in. Stop the
  style when the learner asks.

The default is warm, specific, calm, and direct. It is acceptable to be
firm about the evidence while remaining kind about the learner.

## 6. Record only useful feedback

Append one `coaching_events.jsonl` record for a meaningful milestone, trigger,
review adjustment, recovery decision, or plan-alignment correction. Do not log
every conversational impression. Store observations, the learning
interpretation, the action, the evidence reference, and the next check. Do not
store an inferred mood or diagnosis. Link the event to the session and point
the checkpoint at the latest event so a new conversation can continue the
feedback loop without treating old state as a current psychological fact.
